# The Lantern That Shouldn't Be Lit

## 1. After the Fair

By the time Poppy and Willow left the lantern fair, rain had begun to collect in the paper pumpkins hanging above the square. Their candlelight shone through the wet orange folds and trembled in the puddles below, where scraps of ribbon floated among the apple stalks. One pumpkin had sagged beneath the weight of the water, leaving a cheerful mouth beneath a rather unfortunate nose.

Poppy reached up to straighten it, balancing on the edge of the fountain so that her cape brushed the water.

“Leave it,” Willow said. “You’ll pull the whole string down.”

“I’m only trying to straighten it,” Poppy said.

“You were trying to help at the cake stall too,” Willow reminded her.

Poppy folded the icing-stained hem of her cape underneath and stepped down. All around them, the fair was being packed into boxes. The baker shook a shower of crumbs from her tablecloth while two neighbours struggled with a trestle table, and the last of the little painted boats bumped together in the fountain. From the open bakery door came a waft of baked apple and cinnamon that made Poppy wish she had saved some of her money.

Willow had tucked her handmade lantern, still unlit, beneath her moss-green coat, where its corners pressed awkwardly against her ribs. She had spent the afternoon cutting out its little windows, and whenever Poppy came too close she turned sideways to protect it.

The baker came to the doorway with two folded blankets over her arm. “If you see the mender and her apprentice, tell them their room’s ready,” she called. “They usually come over the low bridge, but I haven’t seen them yet.”

Poppy promised she would, already hoping the visitors might bring one of the little clockwork animals the apprentice sometimes made from scraps.

They were passing the fountain when a light appeared beyond the gardens, in the mill’s upper window.

“Look,” Poppy said, catching Willow’s sleeve. “Upstairs.”

Alders hid the mill’s lower walls. In summer, water spilled from the wheel in silver threads, and the whole building hummed pleasantly enough to hear from the square. Now the wheel was black and still, its paddles clogged with leaves. It had stopped for winter a week earlier, and Poppy had seen no light upstairs since.

“Bram must still be working,” Willow said, following her gaze.

“But his cottage was dark when we came past,” Poppy said.

“Then he’s still here, isn’t he?” Willow replied, drawing her sleeve free.

As the village clock began to chime the quarter to twelve, a broad head and shoulders passed between the light and the glass. Poppy thought she recognised the high collar of Bram’s green coat, but before she could be sure the figure bent down and disappeared below the windowsill. Something scraped across the floor with a long, uneven groan that carried between two gusts of rain.

“What’s he doing up there?” Poppy asked.

“We could ask him in the morning,” Willow suggested, looking back towards the bakery.

Poppy knew why Willow wanted to go home. At the last fair, Bram had settled them beside his stove with hot cups between their paws and asked whether they knew the story of the mill bell. The cottage had smelled of drying socks and orange peel, and it had been easy to lean forward while he lowered his voice.

If the mill bell rang after midnight, he had told them, someone would be missing before morning. The mill bell was the little brass bell he kept above the mill fireplace, with a leather strap so that he could carry it to the bank when the water rose.

When Poppy asked who, Bram took his time choosing another log for the stove.

“That depends who’s listening,” he had said.

Willow had refused to use the mill lane for a week afterwards. Poppy had taken it twice that week, although both visits had been in daylight and in the company of somebody considerably larger than herself.

“It was a story,” Willow said now, as though Poppy had mentioned it aloud.

“I know it was,” Poppy replied, keeping her eyes on the window.

The light moved once more and vanished, leaving them with their own dim reflections in a puddle at their feet. Without it, the mill seemed to have drawn back into the trees. Poppy could hear the stream rushing behind the garden wall, much louder than it had been on their way to the fair.

“He might need a hand,” Poppy said, already picking her way down the mill lane between the puddles.

Willow stayed beneath the dripping pumpkins for a moment before she tucked the lantern farther inside her coat and followed. The lane curled behind Bram’s cottage and continued towards the mill. No light showed beneath the cottage’s steep roof. Along the garden fence, wet rosemary brushed their knees and shed its sharp scent whenever Poppy caught a branch. By the time they reached the mill porch, the warmth of the square had given way to the smell of damp wood and the steady dripping of the eaves.

A narrow trail of pale shavings lay beneath the mill’s side window. Rain had flattened the outer edge into the mud, but the wood tucked against the wall was still dry. Poppy could smell it when the wind turned, a fresh, sharp smell that did not belong to the wet leaves.

The front door stood ajar. A strip of green cloth was caught beneath the latch, its loose threads stirring whenever the wind found the gap.

Poppy lifted it into the light from the doorway and recognised the crooked yellow stitches along its edge. Bram had let her mend the cuff of his green coat in the summer, sitting patiently with his arm on the kitchen table while she tried to make the two sides meet. She had hoped nobody else would notice how unevenly she had done it.

“Bram?” she called, easing the cloth from the iron catch.

Her voice travelled into the mill and received no answer. Willow pushed the door wider, revealing an oil lamp burning at the foot of the stairs and muddy tracks across the floor. She took the lantern from beneath her coat and stepped inside, keeping its paper sides clear of the lamp as she touched her candle’s wick to the oil flame. Light filled the little windows she had cut so carefully, and she held the lantern clear of the door as Poppy squeezed past. Above the cold fireplace, the iron hook that held the mill bell was empty, with a pale outline on the wall where its leather strap usually hung.

Something knocked against the wall beyond the porch. Poppy waited with the torn cloth caught between her paws, but no footsteps came down the stairs.

## 2. What Had Left the Mill

The village clock began to strike twelve. As its last chime faded, a bell rang beyond the stream. Poppy tightened her grip on the torn strip of green cloth until cold water squeezed between her paws. She knew that bell well enough to recognise the slight rattle that followed each stroke. There was a small chip in its lip; she had caught a claw on it once while helping Bram polish the brass. The sound came from beyond the stream, and with the empty hook in front of her it sounded wrong.

Willow turned up the lamp and lifted it from the stair. A little smoke curled from the wick before the flame steadied, bringing the mill’s lower room into view. Bram worked here, though he lived in the cottage beside the mill. There was the wooden crate Bram sat on to change his boots, with a bundle of reeds tucked behind it and the dented watering can he had been promising to mend since spring. Everything was familiar except the mud. Deep prints crossed the boards, and at the foot of the stairs one heel had slipped far enough to leave a brown smear against the first riser.

“He went out in a hurry,” Poppy said.

“He might have been coming in,” Willow pointed out. “We don’t know yet.”

Poppy looked at the cloth and imagined Bram being pulled backwards through the doorway, trying to catch hold of something while his sleeve tore. Things like that happened in the Night Owl’s detective stories, which she borrowed from the village shelf. The picture came so readily that she almost told Willow, but she had only the picture, not a reason.

They climbed to the upper room, where the air smelled of old grain and the sweet, dusty straw packed between the rafters. Rain tapped against the window above an open storage chest. Its lid rested against the wall, and two clean tracks ran across the dusty boards from the place where the chest usually stood. The chest stood across the narrow space beneath the sloping roof, hiding the floor behind it. Inside, among folded sacks and a few coils of twine, a pale circle showed where Bram kept his long rope.

Poppy went to the window while Willow held the lamp behind her. At first she could see only the flame and her own wide eyes, but when she cupped her paws around her face the far bank emerged between the reflections. Something pale shifted among the branches, disappeared, then moved again in the same place.

“Can you see him?” Willow asked.

“No, it’s only a branch,” Poppy said at last, drawing away from the cold glass.

Downstairs, they found Bram’s map spread across his desk beneath a jar of pencils. Its corners had curled with age, and the stream wandered through the middle in a thin blue line, much tidier than the water they could hear outside. Somebody had crossed out the low footbridge in thick black ink and drawn a new route up the valley, past a crossing labelled *Alder Arch* and back along the opposite hillside.

Willow passed the oil lamp to Poppy and lifted the map. “Someone’s drawn something on here,” she said, bringing it closer to the light.

Poppy leaned over her shoulder. Whoever had crossed out the bridge had pressed so hard that the pen had nearly worn through the paper.

“Wouldn’t he have left us a note?” she asked.

Beside the map lay a sheet headed *Please don’t use the*. The words stopped in the middle of the line, with the rest of the line left blank. Bram’s pen lay across the page, its nib resting in a dark blot that had spread into the paper fibres.

“Please don’t use the,” Poppy read aloud.

“The what?” Willow asked.

Poppy looked from the unfinished words to the crossed-out bridge.

“He hasn’t said.”

Willow checked that the map’s ink was dry, folded it along its old creases and slipped it inside her coat. Poppy repeated the unfinished words to herself. Before they left, she laid the wet cloth along the edge of the desk, clear of the papers, so Bram would see it if he came back.

Outside, a loose shutter knocked against the wall beside the porch. Poppy watched it strike the stone again and recognised the sound they had heard from the doorway. The lamp picked out a trail of bootprints leading from the porch towards the stream. Rain had softened their edges. Beside the porch, however, a circular mark lay deep in the mud beneath the shelter of the eaves. It was about the width Poppy remembered the bell’s rim being.

She crouched beside it. A little gap interrupted the edge of the circular mark.

“Could that be from the bell?” she asked, holding the lamp low. “There’s a chip in the lip.”

Willow bent to look.

“It could be,” she said. “Let’s see if there’s another.”

They found a second circular mark beneath the reeds, where the overhanging leaves had kept off some of the rain. A pair of deeper bootprints stood beside it. Water had begun to gather in the heels, reflecting two small flames when Poppy passed the lamp over them.

“Someone must have put something down here,” Willow said.

Poppy looked towards the trees beyond the stream. She tried to picture Bram carrying the bell, but from where she stood she could not see where the prints led next.

A rustling further along made Poppy straighten so quickly that her knees hurt, but the light showed only reed heads brushing against one another. She walked on with Willow and tried not to keep looking behind them.

The tool shed stood open beside the path. Poppy passed the oil lamp to Willow so she could lift it above the workbench. Its roof dripped onto a row of empty flowerpots, and in the lamplight they saw Bram’s spare boots waiting neatly beneath his workbench. Poppy looked behind a pile of sacks before she could stop herself.

“That sounded higher up,” Willow said when the bell rang again, turning towards the trees above the far bank.

“Bram says sound travels strangely in the rain,” Poppy replied.

She wished she had not said his name. Willow was still listening, with one ear turned towards the woods, and neither of them could stop listening for the next stroke.

## 3. The Wrong Path

Beyond the shed, the path narrowed between the stream and a steep bank threaded with hazel roots. Willow blew out her paper lantern and let the wick cool before tucking it beneath her coat. She handed the oil lamp back to Poppy so she could unfold the map. On a walk here before the rain, Poppy had kicked through dry yellow leaves while Willow complained about the ones that landed in her boots. Now those leaves lay plastered against the roots in sodden heaps, and the lower stepping stones had disappeared beneath brown water.

Poppy kept the lamp close to the ground so she could follow the prints. Each time she found another, a little of her certainty returned. She was sure Bram had walked this way, carrying the bell, and that if they stayed with the marks they would come to wherever he had gone.

“Poppy, if Bram crossed out the bridge, he may have meant us to avoid it,” Willow said, sheltering the map beneath an overhanging branch.

“Perhaps he didn't want anyone following him,” Poppy replied, watching the light creep into the next footprint.

Willow looked up from the map, and Poppy heard how it sounded: as if Bram hadn’t wanted *them* to follow. She had meant someone else, but she couldn’t say who.

“We should go back,” Willow said. “There are still people in the square. Someone might know where he went.”

Before Poppy could answer, wood knocked against wood around the bend. She hurried towards the noise. “Poppy!” Willow called behind her, still trying to fold the wet map without tearing it.

The bridge came into view through a curtain of willow branches. Its near end was still fixed to the bank, and for a moment Poppy saw the familiar handrail and felt foolish for being afraid. Then the light reached the middle, where two planks were missing and a broken rail dipped into the water. Each time the current lifted it, it struck the underside of the bridge with a dull, hollow knock.

Poppy stepped closer, trying to see whether the footprints continued onto the remaining boards. Under her boot, the mud shifted with a soft sucking sound, and before she could draw back something caught her hard across the shoulders.

Willow had seized her cape. Poppy stumbled against her as the lamp swung sideways, throwing a great yellow arc across the roots and water. A piece of bank broke away from the place where she had been standing and slid into the stream, carrying a tuft of grass that turned over once before it vanished.

They backed along the path with Willow still gripping the cape. Only when they reached the hazel bank did she let go, and even then Poppy could feel where the cloth had pulled tight beneath her chin.

“I was trying to tell you,” Willow said, her voice unsteady. “You didn't even wait.”

Poppy looked down at the lamp instead of answering. Mud speckled its glass, and her paws shook as she tried to wipe it away with the clean part of her cuff.

“I'm sorry,” she said. “I thought I could see where he'd gone.”

Willow stood close enough for their shoulders to touch while she caught her breath. When she took out the map again, Poppy held the lamp above it and waited for her to find the bridge.

“If Bram made that mark, he may have known there was something wrong with the bridge,” Willow said.

“The message wasn't finished either,” Poppy replied, remembering the pen lying in its own ink. “It looks as though whoever wrote it left in a hurry.”

They listened for the bell, but for a while all they heard was the stream working at the bridge. Water slapped the boards, loose wood knocked against the supports, and rain moved through the leaves above them in sudden waves. When the ringing finally came, Poppy turned towards the far bank, then looked over her shoulder because she could no longer be sure where it had begun.

On their way back, Willow found the entrance to the higher path beneath a leaning wooden fingerpost. Moss had grown thickly over the lettering, leaving only *Arch* clear at the end. Behind it, brambles stretched from one side of the track to the other, their hooked stems gleaming where the lamp touched them. Further in, a heavy branch lay across the path, with smaller limbs caught in the undergrowth beyond it.

“We aren't going in there on our own,” Willow said, folding the map away.

Poppy raised the lamp for one last look. Nothing moved beyond the thorns, though the woods seemed to go on a great deal further than they had in daylight. She waited until Willow was ready, and together they followed the near-bank path back past the mill towards the village.

## 4. A Bell in Two Places

The bakery windows were still bright when they reached the square. Steam had clouded the upper panes, and through the open door Poppy could see a tray of buns cooling beneath a cloth. Two neighbours were carrying the last tables inside while the baker held the door with her hip, calling instructions over the scrape of the legs on the stones.

Poppy tried to tell her everything at once. She began with the torn strip of green cloth they had left at the mill, then jumped to the missing bell, and she was halfway through describing the muddy floor when Willow interrupted to explain about the bridge. As soon as the baker understood, she stepped away from the door and guided them under the awning.

“Stay here, both of you,” she said. “I'll get someone.”

Within a few minutes, villagers were gathering with lanterns and coats pulled over their fair clothes. One neighbour went to Bram's cottage beside the mill, and the ferryman arrived with a saw. The ordinary clatter of getting ready steadied Poppy enough to spread the map across a flour sack without spilling the cup of warm apple juice the baker had set beside her.

“I heard that bell,” the baker said. “Just after the clock struck twelve. Down towards the footbridge.”

“I heard it up on the hillside,” the ferryman told her, setting the saw against the wall. “Above the bend.”

Poppy looked from one to the other. She could picture the path between the bridge and the trees, with the water hiding the lower part of it, but she could no longer picture Bram walking there. In her mind the bell travelled through the dark without him, ringing among branches that would have caught its strap if anybody had been carrying it.

“Are you certain?” Willow asked the ferryman.

“Yes,” he said. “The quarter past twelve had just struck when I finished helping with the crates. I went down to the landing and tied up the punt, and then I heard it. No one's taking a boat out again until that water goes down.”

The baker was certain too. She had been closing her upstairs shutters when the sound reached her and had glanced at the clock, which still showed only a few minutes past midnight. Afterwards, she had come back down to help finish packing away the tables.

“I thought he was playing one of his jokes,” she admitted, looking out towards the mill.

Willow smoothed a crease in the map. The longer she studied it, the more Poppy wanted her to say something, even if it was only to ask another question.

“What are you thinking?” Poppy asked.

“Were you still helping with the crates when she shut her shutters?” Willow asked the ferryman.

“That's right,” he replied. “She called goodnight from upstairs. We still had the last stack to put away.”

“So she heard it before you'd finished the crates, and you heard it after,” Willow said. “Someone might have had time to carry it uphill. But sound travels strangely in the rain. Those accounts don't prove the bell moved.”

Poppy heard the words again, this time with a space between the two sounds. There might have been footsteps in that space, even if nobody had seen who was walking.

“It sounded higher up when we were at the shed too,” Willow added. She placed a claw beside the footbridge on the map and traced the path up the far bank until she reached a hatched patch marked *Quarry Shelter*. Poppy leaned closer, seeing for the first time that the little shape above the lettering was a roof.

“You think Bram carried it up there?” she asked.

“He might have done,” Willow replied. “If he crossed before the bridge broke.”

“The old shelter's above the flood line,” the ferryman said, bending over the map. “If he reached that bank, that's where he could have gone to get clear of the water.”

“Can we get there?” the baker asked.

“Round by Alder Arch,” he said. “It's the high stone bridge further up the gorge, well above the water. We climb on this bank, cross there and come back along the opposite hillside. I walked the lower stretch of the approach this afternoon, as far as the old steps.”

“There are brambles right across the track,” Poppy told him. “And a heavy branch beyond them, with smaller ones caught in the bushes.”

“That sounds like a limb from the beech beside the steps,” he said. “It leaned right over that path. We'll need the saw.”

The neighbour returned from Bram's cottage while they were looking at the route. Nobody had answered the door, but through the kitchen window she had seen three clean bowls beside a covered pot, with chairs drawn up ready at the table.

“He was expecting the mender and her apprentice,” the baker said, pressing a floury paw to her forehead. “He told me he'd kept some supper for them. I'd forgotten.”

“They'd have come towards the footbridge, wouldn't they?” Poppy asked.

“Usually,” the ferryman said. “I didn't bring them over. My last passengers were across before the fair ended.”

“Perhaps he went out to meet them,” Poppy said.

“We know he was expecting them,” Willow replied. “We don't know whether he found them.”

Poppy looked down at the little roof on the map. Bram might be up there with two other people, listening for someone to come, while she had been looking behind sacks and imagining the worst.

“We should try the shelter,” she said. She moved her cup clear of the paper and folded the map along its old creases before passing it to the ferryman.

“We'll go together,” he told her. “Keep close, both of you, and let us clear the way.”

## 5. An Answer in the Dark

Before they left, the baker put a lantern in her window for anyone returning by the village road. A neighbour brought folded canvas and two stout poles from the fair stalls. Poppy followed the ferryman between the gardens, with Willow beside her and the other lamps bobbing behind. At the last turn, the warm square in the bakery window disappeared among the trees.

Beneath the brambles were old stone steps, edged with ferns and worn into shallow hollows. The adults cut back the thorns and passed the stems carefully from paw to paw. Beyond the steps, a fallen limb of the beech blocked the narrow track. Its trunk leaned into the bushes, with smaller branches caught beneath it.

Just before the obstruction, a broad rock overlooked the opposite slope. The ferryman checked the ground before letting Poppy and the baker stand beside it. Across the gorge, a bell rang three times. Poppy raised the oil lamp she had carried from the mill and found a small light beneath a rocky overhang.

“Bram!” Poppy called, with both paws cupped beside her mouth.

The ringing stopped. Far below, a branch crashed against the stones, and for a moment the rush of water seemed to fill the whole valley. Then three more strokes came through the rain.

Poppy wanted to call that an answer. But the bell had been ringing before she shouted too.

“We can see your light!” she called. She raised and lowered her lamp twice, waited, then did it once more. Across the gorge, the small light moved twice, paused, and moved again.

“They can see us,” Willow said.

A voice followed, broken by the water and the distance. Poppy caught “three of us”, then something she could not understand, and finally “can't walk”. She could not recognise the speaker.

“Stay at the shelter!” the ferryman called. “We're coming round!”

The ferryman returned to the fallen limb, where the saw began its steady rasp. From the rock, the shelter looked almost close enough to touch. Between it and Poppy's lamp, the gorge fell away into darkness full of water.

Willow brought out her paper lantern. She had blown out its candle before the narrow path beyond the shed and tucked it beneath her coat; the corners had crumpled when she pulled Poppy away from the bank. Now she eased them apart and lit the wick from the oil lamp. Amber light filled the little windows.

“I can hold the map for you,” she told the ferryman. “There's another fork after Alder Arch.”

He passed it to her. “Stay between us, and tell me when we're near the turning.”

Poppy wanted to go too. She had followed the prints and called across the gorge, and she wanted to see who would stand up when the rescuers arrived. Then the shelter light dipped out of sight. She lifted her lamp, and it rose again.

“I'll stay here,” she said. “They'll know we haven't gone away.”

The baker stayed with Poppy while the others rolled the sawn length clear. Wet sawdust clung to the ferryman's boots. Beyond the obstruction, the lamps began to climb, disappearing behind trees and appearing again a little higher.

Whenever the shelter light moved, Poppy answered. Between signals, the baker helped her rest the lamp on the broad rock and wrapped a dry scarf around her shoulders. Poppy could feel the warmth gathering beneath her wet cape.

Further up the gorge, the search party appeared in a row across the high stone bridge. Willow's small paper lantern travelled between two larger lamps. Then the opposite hillside hid them.

Poppy watched until her eyes watered. At last, new lights gathered beneath the overhang. A voice called that they had found Bram and both travellers. The mender had hurt her ankle. They were bringing her back.

Later, Willow would tell Poppy how Bram had risen when the lamps reached the shelter. His coat was muddy, and the mender sat with her ankle wrapped beside the apprentice's work case. A long rope ran between two trees along the slippery slope below them.

Bram said he had fixed it there so the apprentice could help the mender climb to the overhang. It had given them something to hold, but it could not make her able to walk the steep path to Alder Arch.

As the neighbours unfolded their canvas, Bram began talking about the bridge. “The storm took the support away. I hadn't been down to the crossing since yesterday morning. I knew nothing was wrong until I heard them calling tonight.”

The mender looked up. “But you stopped us there this afternoon. You were on the far approach. You told us to take Alder Arch because you were doing work underneath.”

“I meant I hadn't crossed it,” Bram said quickly. He bent to help with the canvas before she could answer.

Willow held the lamp steady while the ferryman secured the stretcher. She did not interrupt. But she kept the two statements separate in her mind: Bram had said he had not been to the crossing; the mender said he had spoken to her there. They could ask again when everyone was warm.

## 6. Before Morning

The return took much longer than the outward journey. The neighbours carried the stretcher over the roots, stopping on the steeper stretches to change places. The apprentice walked beside the mender with her work case pressed against her chest. Bram followed with the rope coiled over one shoulder and the bell over the other.

When the lamps reached the rock, Poppy stood so quickly that the baker's scarf slipped to the ground. Bram lowered himself beside her. At his cuff, the crooked yellow stitches dangled from a torn edge.

“We found the green cloth on the latch,” Poppy told him. “I thought someone had taken you.”

“I caught my sleeve as I went out,” he said. “I'm sorry you were frightened.” He put down the bell, and Poppy saw the chip in its lip. Bram noticed her looking. “I rested it by the porch and under the reeds while I tightened the strap.”

That fitted the two circular marks. Poppy felt relief, but Willow was watching Bram's face. On the walk back to the bakery, she quietly told Poppy what he and the mender had said at the shelter.

At the bakery, the mender was settled beside the oven with her ankle supported. Someone fetched the pot from Bram's cottage and heated the supper. Steam smelling of leeks and pepper rose from the bowls, and Poppy wrapped her paws around hers.

The mender explained that Bram had directed them towards Alder Arch that afternoon. They had found the upper approach obstructed and waited in a wayside shed on the far bank, hoping the rain would ease. As the night grew colder and the rain worsened, they decided to try the lower crossing again. Bram had said he was working beneath it, but he had said nothing about the missing supports. They hoped he would have finished by then. On the muddy approach, before they reached the bridge, her ankle turned beneath her.

Bram said he had heard their calls from the mill, taken the rope and bell, and crossed while the middle planks were still in place. After they gave way, he had helped the travellers uphill. Those parts of his account agreed with theirs.

“The bridge was sound yesterday,” he added. “The storm must have done the rest.”

Willow set down her spoon. “You said you knew nothing was wrong until tonight. But you sent them away from the bridge this afternoon.”

“That's what I said,” the mender confirmed. “You told us you were working underneath.”

Bram looked at Poppy. “You know I wouldn't leave anyone out there.”

Poppy did know that he had stayed with the travellers. She wanted that to answer Willow's question, but it answered a different one.

“You helped them,” Poppy said. “But what work were you doing under the bridge?”

Bram pushed his bowl away. “Only clearing old wood. There was nothing worth keeping.”

“If you were only clearing it away, why were there fresh shavings beneath your window?” Poppy asked. “What were you making?”

“Someone had been working on wood,” Willow said. “That doesn’t tell us whether it came from the bridge.” She turned to the ferryman. “Can we look?”

By then the sky was beginning to pale. The mender remained with the baker while the ferryman took the rabbits and Bram to the mill. In the upper room, behind the displaced chest, lay three squared lengths of timber. Pale curls covered the boards beneath a small plane. A narrow opening in the side wall looked down on the shavings Poppy had seen outside.

The ferryman turned one length in the lamplight. Beneath a newly shaved strip, a blue triangle remained beside two square bolt holes. “I marked the crossing's braces with these when we repaired it last spring,” he said. “The marks faced underneath. This was a support, not loose scrap.”

Willow asked whether it might have been replaced during a repair. The ferryman shook his head. “No replacements were authorised. We'll check the other end before we decide what happened.”

The ferryman left the rabbits in the mill yard with the neighbour and went to inspect the village end of the crossing from firm ground. When he returned, he described an empty pair of bolt holes and a short section of brace still fixed beneath the deck. Its end was flat, with parallel saw marks. There were no torn fibres where the rest should have been.

“The storm may have brought the planks down,” Willow said. “But it doesn't explain the missing bolts and the cut brace. Someone had removed part of the bridge's support.”

Poppy looked towards the upper window. The scraping they had heard did not prove what Bram had been moving. The shavings alone had not proved it either. But the marked timber in his workroom and the cut support at the bridge belonged in the same account.

“It was only old wood,” Bram said.

“It was a support,” the ferryman replied. “It bears the repair mark I put on the braces last spring. No replacements were authorised, and now a brace has been cut away. How did those lengths get into your workroom?”

Bram opened his mouth, then looked away. “Bram?” Poppy said.

He sat on the porch step. After a long silence, he said he had been taking timber from the stores and selling it to pay a debt. That afternoon, he had removed braces from the old crossing too. He had thought the remaining timber would hold.

“When it started to shift, I sent people round by the arch,” he said. “I thought I could replace the braces before anyone looked. Then the rain came.”

He admitted carrying the lengths upstairs and shaving off their repair marks. At quarter to twelve, he had been dragging the chest in front of them when he heard the travellers. He took the rope from the chest, carried the lamp downstairs, crossed out the bridge on his map and began the warning note. Their next call made him leave it unfinished.

“Why didn't you fetch help?” Poppy asked.

Bram looked towards the stream. “I should have fetched someone before I crossed. But then I'd have had to explain about the supports. I told myself I could bring them back before anyone found out. The bridge held when I went over. I thought it would hold long enough to get them home.”

“And then the planks gave way,” Willow said.

Bram nodded. “After that, I couldn't get her across. I kept hoping the water would fall. The longer I waited, the harder it was to admit why we were still there.”

Poppy looked at the rope beside his boots. Bram had gone out to help the travellers. His silence had also delayed the help they needed.

The ferryman took Bram's store keys. Until the village had examined the crossing and the missing timber, Bram would have no access to either. The neighbours closed both approaches to the footbridge. The rescue was over, but there was work left that could not be put right before breakfast.

Back at the bakery, the apprentice opened her work case and offered to mend Willow's lantern. A branch had crushed one window on the return journey. She fitted a piece of amber paper into its frame while Willow held the scissors, and turned it towards the oven to check the edges.

Bram waited beside the door as Poppy and Willow put on their coats. “I was glad to see your lamp,” he told Poppy.

“I'm glad we found you,” she said. After a moment, she added, “I wish you'd told us the truth.”

He had no quick reply. Poppy did not wait for one.

Outside, the unfortunate paper pumpkin still hung over the fountain with its nose folded sideways. Poppy stopped beneath it, then looked at Willow. They were both too tired to climb up. They took the longer way home, sharing the light from the repaired lantern as the smell of morning bread drifted across the square.

Later that week, the village appointed a new caretaker. Bram had to account for the timber he had sold and help pay for the repairs. Poppy saw him carrying boards to the workshop under the ferryman's supervision. She raised a paw, but she did not run over as she once would have done.

That evening, she opened her notebook beside Willow. Under their first guesses, she wrote what they had actually found. Some guesses had been wrong. Some had led them somewhere useful. This time, she left space between the two.
