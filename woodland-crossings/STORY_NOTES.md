# Story development notes

Companion to `STORY.md`. This is a narrative draft, not a question bank or game implementation. The six chapters follow the investigation rather than a prescribed punctuation syllabus. No artwork or game files have been changed.

## Character bible

| Character | Wants and contradictions | Choices that matter | Voice and visual identity |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Pip | Wants to discover something interesting and be useful. Notices small physical details, then makes an explanation before she has enough evidence. Affectionate without being especially good at saying so. | Her curiosity starts the investigation. Her haste brings her too near the damaged bank. She apologises, accepts Mara's lead and stays at the signal point when she wants to accompany the rescue. | Concrete questions, occasional unwarranted certainty, humour through actions rather than constant quips. Compact cream rabbit, one bent ear, plum cape, rust scarf, old satchel. Icing on the cuff provides a small recurring human detail. |
| Mara | Wants her judgement to count. Afraid of the legend but unwilling to accept it without evidence. Can seek certainty so carefully that she postpones action. | Pulls Pip back from the failing bank. Orders the witness accounts by time. Chooses to accompany the adults and use the map rather than remain within sight of the bakery. | Specific, dry, practical. Warmth shows through coming along and protecting others. Taller grey-brown rabbit, long ears, moss-green coat with checked lining. Her handmade paper lantern is cherished, useful and eventually repaired. |
| Bram | Wants to manage things himself. A capable, kindly caretaker whose confidence exceeds his judgement on this occasion. Enjoys a frightening story when everyone is safe beside his stove. | Marks the dangerous route but abandons the warning note to reach the travellers. Misjudges the time available. Stays with the injured mender and cold apprentice, using the bell to seek help. Admits his mistake. | Brief, plain speech. Broad badger in a green coat, one cuff repaired badly by Pip in yellow thread. His vulnerability becomes visible without making him helpless. |
| The mender | Wants to get herself and her apprentice home without becoming a burden. Her discomfort is real but the story does not dwell on injury. | Cannot climb the steep route unsupported, making practical help essential. | A few ordinary, mildly apologetic comments. Species can be fixed during visual development. |
| The apprentice | Wants to protect the work case and help the mender. Has a useful skill rather than merely requiring rescue. | Repairs Mara's paper lantern during the return to warmth. | Minimal dialogue. Tools and careful hands distinguish the character. |

The baker and ferryman are functional supporting characters. The baker brings warmth, a reliable timed observation and company at the signal point. The ferryman knows the upper crossing, brings tools and leads the adults. Neither replaces the rabbits' contribution to locating the stranded group.

## Relationship and tone

The central friendship uses disagreements that have consequences. Pip is not always right because she is the curious protagonist. Mara is not merely a timid companion waiting to become brave. Both take responsibility, and the climactic separation demonstrates mutual trust.

Inspiration is structural rather than imitative: the tactile domestic life of classic woodland books, resourceful young detectives who compare explanations, and Gothic suspense that makes familiar sounds and objects uncertain. The prose is original. No character recreates a recognisable fictional detective.

The visual world should remain predominantly warm storybook woodland: wool, moss, autumn leaves, lamplight, worn wood and handmade paper. Unease comes from a familiar thing in the wrong setting, especially the portable bell, the moving upstairs light and the unfinished warning. Avoid gore, red-eyed monsters or a villain silhouette behind every tree.

## Spatial and temporal continuity

- The village square, bakery and mill are close together. The fair has ended, but adults are still putting it away after midnight.
- The mill stands on the village bank. Its upper window overlooks the stream and far approach to the lower footbridge.
- Bram marks the low route as unsafe because the stream is rising. Hearing the travellers call, he goes upstairs for the long rope, then leaves with the portable bell. This is the movement and scraping Pip and Mara witness from the square.
- The girls' walk around the lane and inspection of the mill give Bram time to reach and cross the still-intact low bridge. His coat catches as he goes out. The door remains ajar, not mysteriously locked.
- The low bridge loses its middle planks after Bram crosses. The mender has injured her ankle on the far approach. All three retreat uphill on the far bank to the quarry shelter.
- The girls follow the near-bank trail, discover the collapse and return to the square. They do not cross the broken bridge or enter the water.
- Witnesses heard the bell at different times as the group moved uphill. This directs the organised search towards the shelter.
- The safe alternative climbs along the village bank, crosses the gorge at Alder Arch and returns along the far hillside. Alder Arch is a high stone bridge, not a submerged ford or low causeway. The ferryman crossed it earlier that day.
- A fallen beech obstructs the approach. Adults with tools clear it. The steep return from the shelter requires several people to support and transport the injured traveller. Bram cannot carry the mender and safeguard the apprentice alone.
- Pip and the baker remain on firm ground within sight of the shelter. Mara accompanies the adult search party. The return with the stretcher is slow. The gathering, upper-path rescue, careful return and warming supper occupy the remaining night, with the final scene near dawn. Exact clock times beyond midnight are not specified.

## Fair-play clue ledger

| Clue as encountered | Frightening interpretation available to the reader | Eventual explanation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Light moving behind upstairs shutters | Somebody searches the old mill at night. | Bram looks towards his guests, fetches the rope and carries the lamp downstairs. |
| Heavy scrape upstairs | A struggle or something being dragged away. | The storage chest must be moved to reach the long rope. The tracks are visible when the rabbits inspect it. |
| Torn green cloth with yellow stitches | Something has happened to Bram at the doorway. | Bram catches his cuff while hurrying out. Pip recognises the repair she made. |
| Empty hook and missing carrying strap | Someone stole the bell or took it for a sinister purpose. | Bram carries it for warning and signalling. The hook is intact. |
| Midnight bell and Bram's old story | The legend is being fulfilled. | A real emergency produces the circumstances of the old warning story. |
| Bootprints and round dents | Someone has taken a heavy object towards the stream. | Bram rests the portable bell as he carries it. There is no invented second set of abductors' footprints. |
| Crossed-out bridge and unfinished warning | A route has been forbidden or a warning interrupted. | Bram knows the low route is becoming dangerous and misjudges how long his errand will take. |
| Bootprints end at water beside broken bridge | Bram has vanished or fallen into the flood. | He crossed while it was intact. Later bell locations offer evidence that he continued uphill. |
| Witnesses place the bell in different locations | One witness is wrong or the bell moves impossibly. | The observations were not simultaneous. Mara asks about sequence. |
| Roof symbol on the map, three bowls at the cottage | Bram had plans involving other people. | He expected the mender and apprentice for supper. The marked shelter offers a search destination. |
| Bell replies after Pip calls | Somebody is alive and deliberately answering. | The missing group can hear the rescuers. The mystery turns decisively into a rescue. |

Every major ominous clue receives an ordinary explanation. The draft does not append a supernatural sting that would undo those explanations. The legend retains its emotional force because the danger was real even though there was no monster.

## Dialogue revision

The current draft uses explicit speech tags throughout so the reader can follow each speaker. Short, cryptic exchanges were revised into ordinary spoken phrasing, with actions retained where they reveal hesitation or emotion. The bridge disagreement, reunion and closing conversation have fuller responses. Plot and clue logic are unchanged.
