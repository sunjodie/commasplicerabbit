# Mystery question bank

30 story questions. Each has one complete target under its editing brief, visible whole-text goals and these shared rules. Grammatical alternatives receive neutral brief feedback.

**Shared rules:** Use the pieces provided. Keep correct commas and other wording. Add only the named joining words at the relationship described; split only where the brief asks.

Paired recovery questions are in content.js and appear only after support was needed.

## 1. Chapter 1 · scene-time-shift

**Brief:** Start a new sentence when the account moves from the past to the present evening. Keep every word.

**Whole-text goal:** 2 sentence(s); no added joining words.

**Original**

The mill had stood empty since last winter, tonight, a lantern glowed in its upper window.

**Target**

The mill had stood empty since last winter. Tonight, a lantern glowed in its upper window.

**Why:** The two clauses are complete. A new sentence marks the change in time; a semicolon would be grammatical but would not meet this brief.

## 2. Chapter 1 · concession-opening

**Brief:** Keep this as one sentence. Change punctuation only if the structure requires it.

**Target**

Although every door was locked, the light above the waterwheel kept moving.

**Why:** Although introduces a dependent opening. Keep its comma; the main clause supplies the ending.

## 3. Chapter 1 · paired-observations

## 4. Chapter 1 · noun-list

## 5. Chapter 1 · time-opening-paired-main

## 6. Chapter 2 · additive-independent

**Brief:** Use AND to combine the two discoveries into one sentence.

**Whole-text goal:** 1 sentence(s); add only and.

**Original**

Pip found a deep print beside the gate, Mara noticed a round dent farther along the bank.

**Target**

Pip found a deep print beside the gate, and Mara noticed a round dent farther along the bank.

**Why:** Both discoveries are complete clauses. Comma + and adds the second one.

## 7. Chapter 2 · contrast-independent

**Brief:** Use BUT to contrast what the print suggested with what Pip could see.

**Whole-text goal:** 1 sentence(s); add only but.

**Original**

The print looked fresh, nobody was visible on the path ahead.

**Target**

The print looked fresh, but nobody was visible on the path ahead.

**Why:** Comma + but expresses the contrast between the clear mark and the missing person. Yet could be grammatical too; this brief specifies but.

## 8. Chapter 2 · cause-result

**Brief:** Use SO to make the second clause the result of the first.

**Whole-text goal:** 1 sentence(s); add only so.

**Original**

The trail of round dents continued beyond the gate, Pip followed the marks towards the reeds.

**Target**

The trail of round dents continued beyond the gate, so Pip followed the marks towards the reeds.

**Why:** The first discovery leads to the second action. Comma + so makes the result explicit.

## 9. Chapter 2 · alternative-consequences

**Original**

**Target**

They could follow the prints now, or they could wait until the fog lifted.

**Why:** Comma + or connects two complete clauses offering alternatives.

## 10. Chapter 2 · participial-subject

## 11. Chapter 3 · explanatory-for

**Brief:** Use FOR to introduce the explanation. Keep this as one sentence.

**Whole-text goal:** 1 sentence(s); add only for.

**Original**

Mara suspected that the bell had been carried away, its circular base matched the marks by the stream.

**Target**

Mara suspected that the bell had been carried away, for its circular base matched the marks by the stream.

**Why:** Comma + for introduces the reason for the suspicion. Because is another grammatical way to express a reason; this brief asks for for.

## 12. Chapter 3 · negative-inversion

**Target**

The bell had not fallen into the stream, nor had anyone left it in the tool shed.

**Why:** Nor adds another negative clause. The auxiliary verb comes before its subject.

## 13. Chapter 3 · unexpected-yet

**Target**

**Why:** Comma + yet contrasts an undamaged mounting with the missing bell. But would also be grammatical; yet is the requested word.

## 14. Chapter 3 · embedded-that-clauses

**Brief:** Keep one sentence and every word. Link the findings with punctuation alone.

**Whole-text goal:** 1 sentence(s); no added joining words.

**Target**

Pip knew that the bell could be lifted from its frame; Mara remembered that the caretaker kept a carrying strap nearby.

## 15. Chapter 3 · relative-subject-main

**Brief:** Keep the description inside the first clause. Link the two complete observations in one sentence with punctuation alone.

**Whole-text goal:** 1 sentence(s); no added joining words.

**Original**

The scrap of cloth, which was caught on a nail, matched a patch on the caretaker’s coat, a green thread still clung to the nail.

**Target**

The scrap of cloth, which was caught on a nail, matched a patch on the caretaker’s coat; a green thread still clung to the nail.

**Why:** This comma opens extra information about the cloth. This comma closes the extra information; “matched” completes the main clause. The cloth’s match and the remaining thread are described in complete clauses. Use a semicolon.

## 16. Chapter 4 · however-transition

**Brief:** Keep one sentence and all words. Repair the join before HOWEVER using punctuation only.

**Whole-text goal:** 1 sentence(s); no added joining words.

**Original**

The map had been altered, however, the original route was still visible beneath the new line.

**Target**

The map had been altered; however, the original route was still visible beneath the new line.

**Why:** However is not a FANBOYS conjunction. Use a semicolon before it; its following comma stays.

## 17. Chapter 4 · therefore-transition

**Brief:** Keep the BECAUSE opening attached and preserve one sentence. Repair the remaining join with punctuation only.

**Whole-text goal:** 1 sentence(s); no added joining words.

**Original**

Because the fresh line avoided the upstream bridge, Pip suspected danger there, therefore, she checked the older map.

**Target**

Because the fresh line avoided the upstream bridge, Pip suspected danger there; therefore, she checked the older map.

**Why:** Because introduces a dependent opening. Therefore introduces a conclusion, but does not join complete clauses by itself. Use a semicolon before it.

## 18. Chapter 4 · nevertheless-transition

**Brief:** Keep the WHICH description enclosed inside the first clause. Preserve one sentence and all words; repair the remaining join with punctuation only.

**Target**

The old map, which had been left beside an open window, was damp at the edges; nevertheless, the hillside turning remained clear.

**Why:** Open the extra information about the map. Close the extra information before the main verb. Nevertheless is a transition. A semicolon links these complete clauses.

## 19. Chapter 4 · condition-contrast

**Target**

If the old map was accurate, the lower path led straight to the bridge, but the new line climbed towards the hillside.

## 20. Chapter 4 · record-scene-change

**Target**

## 21. Chapter 5 · witness-relative-and-result

**Brief:** Keep the witness description in its sentence. Use SO to connect the blocked view with its consequence. Leave correct commas alone.

**Whole-text goal:** 2 sentence(s); add only so.

**Original**

The baker, who was closing her shutters, heard the bell near the mill. Fog covered the lane, she could not see who was carrying it.

**Target**

The baker, who was closing her shutters, heard the bell near the mill. Fog covered the lane, so she could not see who was carrying it.

## 26. Chapter 6 · passage-scene-and-reason

**Brief:** Keep the opening attached. Begin a new sentence at the change from setting out to hearing voices. Use FOR to introduce the evidence for the final judgement.

**Whole-text goal:** 4 sentence(s); add only for.

**Original**

After Pip and Mara compared the accounts, they left the village, on the hillside, they heard voices through the trees. The callers were close, their words carried clearly above the rain.

**Target**

After Pip and Mara compared the accounts, they left the village. On the hillside, they heard voices through the trees. The callers were close, for their words carried clearly above the rain.

## 27. Chapter 6 · passage-observation-and-result

**Brief:** Keep the WHO description and the WHILE opening attached. Use SO for the result of the broken bridge. Keep all other sentence boundaries.

**Whole-text goal:** 3 sentence(s); add only so.

**Original**

The caretaker, who was wrapping a blanket around a traveller, looked up as Pip arrived. The footbridge had lost two planks, he had led the travellers along the higher path.

While the storm passed, they waited beneath the mill’s old porch.

**Target**

The caretaker, who was wrapping a blanket around a traveller, looked up as Pip arrived. The footbridge had lost two planks, so he had led the travellers along the higher path.

While the storm passed, they waited beneath the mill’s old porch.

**Why:** Open the extra information about the caretaker. Close the description before the main verb. So makes the damaged crossing the cause of choosing a safer route. While introduces a dependent opening.

## 28. Chapter 6 · passage-warning-and-contrast

**Brief:** Keep the two paragraphs and existing sentence boundaries. Use BUT for the first contrast. Make the join before THEREFORE grammatical without adding words or a sentence.

**Whole-text goal:** 3 sentence(s); add only but.

**Original**

The travellers could not see the damaged bridge, they could see the lantern in the mill’s upper window.

The light marked the danger, therefore, the caretaker had left it burning while he carried the bell upstream. The bell warned anyone who had missed the light.

**Target**

The travellers could not see the damaged bridge, but they could see the lantern in the mill’s upper window.

The light marked the danger; therefore, the caretaker had left it burning while he carried the bell upstream. The bell warned anyone who had missed the light.

**Why:** But contrasts the lack of a view with the visible warning light. Therefore needs a semicolon before it when the clauses stay in one sentence.

## 29. Chapter 6 · passage-reassurance-and-choice

**Brief:** Keep the ALTHOUGH opening. Use YET to contrast the threatening clues with their reassuring explanation, and OR to offer the travellers a choice.

**Whole-text goal:** 3 sentence(s); add only yet, or.

**Original**

Although the coat was torn, the caretaker was unhurt. The missing bell and altered map had looked threatening, both had helped the travellers reach safety.

They could wait for the rain to stop, they could return with Pip by the hillside route.

**Target**

Although the coat was torn, the caretaker was unhurt. The missing bell and altered map had looked threatening, yet both had helped the travellers reach safety.

They could wait for the rain to stop, or they could return with Pip by the hillside route.

**Why:** Although introduces a dependent opening. Yet highlights the unexpected contrast. Or offers two complete alternatives; and would make them cumulative instead.

## 30. Chapter 6 · final-case-report

**Brief:** Edit the whole report. Keep dependent openings and extra-information commas. Keep the paired physical clues in one sentence using punctuation only. Use SO for the rescue’s result. Begin a new sentence at “By dawn”. Add no other words.

**Whole-text goal:** 5 sentence(s); add only so.

**Original**

Although the upper room had been locked for years, its lantern was burning for a reason. The portable bell, which the caretaker had carried upstream, had left a round rim in the mud, his patched coat had shed a thread beside the frame.

The light and ringing warned the travellers, they avoided the damaged bridge. The altered map showed the safer path, by dawn, everyone was waiting under the mill’s porch.

**Target**

Although the upper room had been locked for years, its lantern was burning for a reason. The portable bell, which the caretaker had carried upstream, had left a round rim in the mud; his patched coat had shed a thread beside the frame.

The light and ringing warned the travellers, so they avoided the damaged bridge. The altered map showed the safer path. By dawn, everyone was waiting under the mill’s porch.

**Why:** Although introduces the opening; its comma stays. This comma opens extra information about the bell. This comma closes that information before “had left”. The bell’s mark and the coat’s trace are described in complete, closely related clauses. Use a semicolon. So connects the warning and the successful result. By dawn changes the time and begins the closing scene. Use a full stop.
